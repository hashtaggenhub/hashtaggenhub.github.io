How hashtags actually work in 2026

A hashtag is a label, and labels only help when they describe what is genuinely in the post. Every major platform now reads far more than your hashtags — captions, on-screen text, audio, watch time and saves all feed the recommendation systems — but hashtags still do one job nothing else does as cleanly: they file your post into a topic shelf where people who follow or search that topic can find it. Think of them as the index card, not the book.

That is why the generator above is built the way it is. For each topic it starts from a hand-checked list of tags that people in that niche really use, then layers in variations of your exact keyword — compound forms, community forms and everyday phrasings — so the set reads like something a person in that community would write, not a wall of generic spam tags. A set for "trail running" should not look like a set for "wedding photography", and with a good generator, it doesn't.

Hashtags also behave differently on each platform, which is why the tool asks where you are posting before it builds your set. Instagram treats hashtags as a discovery and categorisation signal on posts and Reels. TikTok leans on them lightly — search and topic pages matter, but the first seconds of the video matter more. YouTube uses a small number of hashtags above the title as topic markers. X rewards restraint: one or two precise tags beat a paragraph of them. Our guide on how many hashtags to use on each platform walks through the current numbers in detail.

One more thing worth knowing before you copy anything: hashtags are not a growth hack by themselves. They put a relevant post in front of a relevant audience; they cannot rescue a post the audience doesn't want. The creators who get consistent results treat hashtags as the last five percent of the work — the hook, the cover frame and the first caption line do the heavy lifting — and then use a tool like this one to label the finished post accurately and quickly.

How many hashtags should you use?

The honest answer is "it depends on the platform", and anyone who gives you one magic number for everywhere is selling you something. Instagram allows up to 30 hashtags on a post, and its own guidance in recent years has pointed creators toward a small, relevant handful — most accounts do well somewhere between 3 and 10 well-chosen tags, with Reels often performing best at the lower end. TikTok posts commonly carry 3 to 6. YouTube displays only the first three hashtags above your video title, which tells you exactly how many it considers useful. On X, one or two is the norm and more than that reads as spam to both readers and the ranking system.

The set-size buttons in the generator (10, 20, 30) exist because many people still prefer to generate a generous pool and then trim it down by hand — that is a perfectly good workflow, especially on Instagram, where you might keep 8 tags on the post and park alternates in your notes for the next one. Mixing big hashtags and niche hashtags

Every hashtag sits somewhere on a spectrum from enormous to tiny. A tag like #travel has been used on hundreds of millions of posts; your photo will be buried under fresh uploads within seconds, but the audience browsing it is vast. A tag like #slowtravelscotland might see a handful of posts a week — small audience, but your post can sit near the top of it for days, in front of exactly the people most likely to care.

Strong sets blend the two ends deliberately: a small number of broad tags to signal the general topic, a solid middle of mid-sized community tags, and a base of specific niche tags where a smaller account can realistically rank. The generator's curated lists are built in those three layers, and the ratio shifts with your set size — a 10-tag set leans niche, a 30-tag set can afford more of the big end. A warning about banned and risky hashtags

Some hashtags are restricted or blocked by platforms because they were overrun by spam or policy-breaking content — and the list is stranger than you would expect, including innocent-looking words that picked up a second meaning somewhere along the way. Posting with a restricted tag can quietly suppress that post's distribution, and repeatedly using flagged tags is one of the common threads in accounts that feel "shadowbanned".

The sets generated here are drawn from lists we keep clean of known restricted tags, but lists age and platforms do not publish a complete official register. Before you reuse any hashtag set for months on end, it is worth learning how to check a hashtag yourself — it takes under a minute per tag, and it is much cheaper than wondering why your reach fell off a cliff.

Instagram, TikTok, YouTube or X — the differences that matter

Because the platforms treat hashtags so differently, a set that is perfect for Instagram is usually wrong for TikTok. Instagram rewards descriptive, topic-true tags and lets you use a fair number of them. TikTok's discovery runs on watch behaviour first; hashtags there work best as search phrases people actually type, kept short. YouTube's three displayed hashtags should be your video's core topic terms, no more. X is a conversation platform — tags join a post to a live discussion, so event and community tags outperform evergreen ones.

When you switch the platform in the generator, the mix changes with it: TikTok sets come out shorter and more search-phrased, X sets lean toward concise CamelCase tags that screen readers can pronounce, and YouTube sets lead with your three strongest topic tags. Getting more from every set you generate

A few habits separate people who get steady value from hashtags from people who copy thirty tags and hope. First, rotate your sets. Posting the identical block under every post trains the platform to treat your tags as boilerplate; keep two or three sets per theme and alternate them. Second, match tags to the specific post, not just your account's general topic — a fitness account posting a meal-prep video should use food-prep tags that day, not its usual gym set. Third, check your results. Every platform shows you, in its analytics, which posts earned reach from non-followers; when a particular tag cluster keeps showing up on your best posts, that is your niche telling you where it lives.

Finally, keep your own list. How Many Hashtags Should You Use in 2026? (Per Platform)

Ask ten creators how many hashtags a post should carry and you will hear everything from "none, they're dead" to "all thirty, every time". Both extremes are wrong, and they are wrong in an instructive way: the right number is not a property of hashtags in general, it is a property of the platform you are posting on, the format you are posting in, and the size of the audience you already have. This guide goes platform by platform, explains the reasoning behind each number, and ends with a simple rule you can apply when you are in doubt.

Why the old advice stopped working

For years the standard advice was to use the maximum Instagram allowed — thirty hashtags — because early studies showed posts with more tags tended to get more engagement. Those studies were measuring a different platform. Instagram's discovery system at the time leaned heavily on hashtag pages; being present on thirty shelves genuinely meant thirty chances to be found. As recommendation systems took over — ranking by predicted interest, watch time, saves and shares — the marginal value of each extra tag fell. A post is now mostly distributed because the system predicts specific people will enjoy it, and hashtags serve as one labelling signal among many. Thirty labels do not make a post thirty times as discoverable; they mostly make it look like it was labelled by a machine, which, increasingly, the system can tell.

There is a second, quieter cost to maxing out: relevance dilution. Every tag you add is a claim about what your post is. Add tags that are only loosely true and you invite the platform to show your post to audiences who will scroll past it — and early indifference is exactly the signal that stops a post spreading. Fewer, truer tags concentrate your post in front of people predisposed to like it.

Instagram: 3 to 10, chosen well

Instagram's own creator guidance in recent years has suggested a small number of relevant hashtags — around three to five — and independent tests since then have broadly agreed that a focused handful performs at least as well as a full block of thirty, with Reels skewing to the lower end. Our practical recommendation: 3 to 10 hashtags for feed posts and carousels, 3 to 5 for Reels. If you enjoy working from a bigger pool, generate 20 or 30 with the hashtag generator and then keep the tags that are genuinely true of that specific post — the discipline of cutting is itself valuable.

Placement matters less than it used to. Tags in the caption and tags in the first comment now perform equivalently for most accounts; choose whichever keeps your caption readable. What does matter is rotation: pasting the identical block under every post for months is associated with declining returns, so keep two or three sets per theme and alternate.

TikTok: 3 to 6, written like search queries

TikTok's For You feed is driven overwhelmingly by watch behaviour, so hashtags there play a supporting role: they help the system categorise the video and help you appear in search results, which have become a real discovery channel as more users search TikTok like a search engine. That search role changes how the tags should read — they should be phrases people type, not community slang. Three to six hashtags is the working range; beyond that you are spending caption space that would do more work as a keyword-rich description of the video. One broad tag, a couple of specific topic phrases, and — only if it is genuinely true — a format tag describing the kind of video it is. We compare TikTok's system with Instagram's in depth in TikTok vs Instagram Hashtags: What Is Different?

YouTube: 3 that matter, up to 15 allowed

YouTube is the clearest platform of all: it displays the first three hashtags above your video's title, which tells you precisely how many it treats as your topic markers. You can add up to fifteen in the description, but the returns past the first few are negligible, and YouTube explicitly ignores all of a video's hashtags if you add more than fifteen or use misleading ones. Use three to five: your core topic, a specific variant of it, and the broader category. Your title, description and chapters do the heavy lifting for YouTube search; hashtags are garnish.

X (Twitter): 1 to 2, and only when they join a conversation

On X, hashtags are not a discovery filing system so much as a way to attach a post to a live conversation — an event, a match, a breaking story, a community's standing tag. One or two is the norm; three or more makes a post read as promotional and measurably reduces how much people engage with it. Write them in CamelCase (#TrailRunning, not #trailrunning) so screen readers pronounce them as words — a small courtesy that also makes the tag easier for everyone to parse at a glance. The generator's X preset applies this automatically.

LinkedIn and Facebook, briefly

LinkedIn behaves like a professional version of the same logic: three to five topical tags help posts surface in topic feeds and searches, and members can follow hashtags, which gives good ones a genuine audience. Facebook hashtags are the weakest of the major platforms — one to three relevant tags do no harm, but reach there comes from groups, shares and watch time, not tag pages.

The rule to remember

If you take one sentence from this guide, take this: use the smallest number of hashtags that honestly covers what the post is, who it is for, and where it belongs. A post about your first marathon needs the marathon, the city, and the beginner-running community — it does not need thirty ways of saying "fitness". Generate generously, publish sparingly, and let the mix of tag sizes do the reach work, as described in Big vs Niche Hashtags: The Mix That Gets Reach. And whatever number you settle on, check the tags themselves against the restricted lists — a single banned tag can undo the whole set, as our banned hashtags guide explains.

Big vs Niche Hashtags: The Mix That Gets Reach

Every hashtag has a size, and size decides what the tag can do for you. Post under a giant hashtag and you are shouting in a stadium: enormous crowd, almost no chance of being heard. Post under a tiny one and you are talking in a small room: few people, but every one of them can hear you clearly and was probably looking for exactly your topic. Neither is "better" — they do different jobs, and a good hashtag set uses both on purpose. This guide explains the three layers of a well-built set and the ratios that suit different account sizes.

What "big" and "niche" actually mean

A hashtag's size is simply how many posts have used it. On Instagram, tags above roughly a million posts behave as "big" tags: the feed of recent posts under them turns over in seconds, so a new post's chance of being seen there is tiny unless it immediately earns exceptional engagement. Tags between about 50,000 and a million posts form the middle band — active communities with real browsing audiences, where a strong post can sit visibly for hours. Below 50,000 posts you are in niche territory: quiet shelves where a relevant post can stay near the top for days and be found by people searching or following that precise interest.

The same logic applies on TikTok and YouTube with views instead of post counts, and the thresholds differ, but the shape is universal: volume and visibility trade against each other. The mistake is choosing one end of the spectrum. All-big sets drown. All-niche sets cap your ceiling — you rank beautifully in rooms too small to grow from.

The three-layer method

Build each set in three layers. Layer one — broad tags (about 20% of the set): one to three large tags that state the general topic. Their job is classification, not traffic: they tell the platform which neighbourhood this post lives in. Layer two — mid-sized community tags (about 40%): the active communities around your topic, where your realistic discovery happens. Layer three — niche tags (about 40%): specific, descriptive tags combining your topic with a place, a style, a level, a sub-interest — the tags under which you can genuinely rank and be followed.

The generator on our homepage is built around exactly this structure: its curated lists hold tags at all three sizes for each topic, and it weights the mix toward the niche end at small set sizes and opens it up as you ask for more. When you trim a generated set by hand, trim from layer one first.

Ratios by account size

The smaller your account, the further your centre of gravity should sit toward the niche end, because ranking is a competition your early engagement numbers have to win. An account under 1,000 followers might run a 10-tag set as one broad tag, three mid-sized and six niche. Between 10,000 and 100,000 followers, a balanced 2–4–4 split works well. Large accounts can invert the logic — their engagement velocity lets them compete under big tags, and niche tags become the garnish rather than the base. If you are in the early stage, the full plan is in our hashtag strategy for small accounts.

Finding your niche tags

Good niche tags are usually compounds: topic + place (#edinburghfood), topic + style (#minimalistwardrobe), topic + audience (#runningformums), topic + level (#beginnerwatercolour). The generator's variation engine builds these forms from your keyword automatically, which is why entering a specific keyword beats a generic one — "trail running" produces far more rankable compounds than "sport". Two more reliable sources: the accounts slightly ahead of you in your niche (their best posts' tags are a tested shortlist), and the platform's own search suggestions, which reveal the phrasings real people type.

What to stop doing

Three habits quietly sabotage otherwise good sets. First, using tags that describe your ambitions rather than your post — a home workout video tagged with stadium-sport tags gets shown to the wrong crowd and stalls. Second, copying a celebrity's set wholesale: their tags work at their engagement scale, not yours. Third, never retiring tags. Review your sets monthly; a tag that has not put a single post in front of non-followers in a month is a slot another tag could be using. Hashtag work is mostly this: small, honest labels, mixed by size, pruned by evidence.

One last caution that applies to every layer: bigger is not the same as safe. Some very large tags have been restricted at various times precisely because they attracted spam, so size is no substitute for checking. Banned Hashtags: How to Check and Avoid Them

Somewhere in your niche, there is probably a perfectly ordinary word that social platforms quietly restrict as a hashtag — because spammers adopted it, because it developed a second meaning, or because it was used to organise content that broke the rules. Use that tag and nothing dramatic happens: no warning, no strike, no notification. Your post simply stops being distributed the way it should be. This guide explains what restricted hashtags are, how to check any tag before you use it, and how to recover if you discover you have been using one.

What "banned" actually means

Platforms rarely use the word "banned" themselves, and there is no complete official list you can download — which is precisely why this catches people out. In practice, restriction comes in grades. Some hashtags are fully blocked: searching them returns nothing or a notice that content is hidden. Others are limited: the tag page exists but shows only a few top posts, or recent posts never appear. And some tags are flagged at the account level when used repeatedly alongside spam-like behaviour, reducing distribution without any single post looking obviously suppressed.

The popular term "shadowban" describes the felt experience — reach falls off a cliff with no explanation — but it is worth being precise: in most cases what people call a shadowban is either a restricted hashtag quietly throttling individual posts, or an account-level trust reduction from behaviour that looks automated (mass following and unfollowing, posting identical comment blocks, using third-party bots). The fix differs, so the diagnosis matters.

How a normal word ends up restricted

Restriction is usually about usage patterns, not the word itself. A tag becomes a magnet for spam or policy-breaking posts; the platform's systems notice that a high share of content under it violates rules; the tag gets limited to break the gathering place. Innocent communities using the same word — a city name, a weekday, a hobby term with an unfortunate double meaning — are collateral damage. This is also why lists of banned hashtags circulating online go stale: tags get restricted, cleaned up and quietly restored, and a list from two years ago will contain both false alarms and missing dangers.

How to check a hashtag in under a minute

You do not need a paid tool; you need sixty seconds and the platform itself. On Instagram, search the tag. If the tag page shows a message that recent posts are hidden because some content may not meet community guidelines, the tag is restricted — do not use it. Also follow the tag temporarily: if the "Recent" view is missing or frozen, treat it as a warning sign. On TikTok, search the tag and look at the view count and the videos shown; a tag whose page will not load, shows unrelated results only, or displays a safety notice is one to avoid. On any platform, a useful cross-check is simply to search the exact tag in a browser with the word "banned" or "restricted" and read recent results — but weigh recent, first-hand reports over recycled listicles.

Build the check into your routine: whenever you create a new hashtag set — including sets from our hashtag generator, whose curated lists we keep clean but which also generates fresh variations of your keyword — spot-check any tag you have never personally used before. Variations you invent yourself deserve the same check; a compound that reads innocently to you may collide with an existing restricted tag.

If you have been using a restricted tag

Do not panic, and do not delete your archive. The practical steps: stop using the tag immediately and remove it from any saved sets and templates — that is where the ongoing damage lives, because templates repeat the mistake on every new post. For recent posts (the last week or two), editing the caption to remove the tag is reasonable where the platform allows it. Then post normally for a couple of weeks with a clean, smaller set — our guide to how many hashtags to use will help you rebuild it — and watch your non-follower reach in analytics. In most hashtag-only cases, distribution recovers on its own once the trigger is gone.

If reach stays depressed across many clean posts, the cause is probably account-level rather than tag-level: review any automation tools connected to your account, stop mass follow/unfollow activity, and use the account-status tools the platforms provide (Instagram's Account Status page, for instance, will tell you plainly if your content is ineligible for recommendation). That page is the ground truth — check it before believing any shadowban rumour, including your own suspicions.

Habits that keep you safe

Keep your sets varied and post-specific rather than one frozen block repeated for months. Avoid engagement-bait tags that promise follow-for-follow or like-for-like exchanges; they cluster around exactly the behaviour platforms police. Be careful with tags in languages you do not read — a borrowed tag may not mean what you think it means. And when a tag suddenly floods with irrelevant content, retire it early: a tag being overrun today is a tag being restricted tomorrow. Prevention here is genuinely cheap; it is the diagnosis after the fact that costs people weeks.

Hashtag Strategy for Small Accounts

If your account has a few hundred or a few thousand followers, most hashtag advice is quietly useless to you, because it is written from the experience of accounts whose posts get thousands of interactions in the first hour. Yours don't — yet — and hashtag strategy is really a game of matching your set to the engagement you can actually generate. This guide lays out a realistic approach for accounts under roughly 10,000 followers: where you can win, what to ignore, and how your sets should evolve as you grow.

The one principle: rank where you can

A hashtag page is a competition ranked by engagement. Under a tag with ten million posts, the visible top spots belong to posts with enormous interaction counts; a small account's post, however good, will not displace them, and its brief appearance in the "recent" stream lasts seconds. Under a tag with 8,000 posts, the maths flips: a post with forty genuine likes and a handful of comments can sit in the top grid for days, being found by exactly the people who follow that interest. For a small account, hashtags are not a lottery ticket — they are a ladder, and you climb it one realistically-sized rung at a time. Our guide to big vs niche hashtags explains the layering in full; for small accounts the practical version is: mostly niche, some mid-sized, one broad tag at most.

Your first ninety days

Weeks 1–4: build the library. Pick the three or four content themes you actually post about. For each, use the hashtag generator with a specific keyword (your exact sub-topic, not the broad category) and save a set of 10–15 tags, weighted to small and mid-sized ones. Check each unfamiliar tag the way our banned hashtags guide describes — two minutes now saves a suppressed month later.

Weeks 5–8: rotate and observe. Alternate between two sets per theme rather than repeating one block, and start noting which posts earn reach from non-followers — every platform's analytics shows this. You are looking for patterns: which theme, which tag cluster, which format. Expect noise; you are looking for repeats, not one lucky post.

Weeks 9–12: promote what works. Tags that keep appearing on your better posts graduate into every set for that theme. Tags that never coincide with non-follower reach get replaced with slightly larger neighbours — the next rung up. This is also when to add one or two mid-sized tags you previously couldn't compete under, because your baseline engagement has grown.

Engagement is the fuel; hashtags are the map

Small accounts often treat hashtags as a substitute for the harder work, so it needs saying plainly: tags route distribution, they do not create it. The behaviours that compound for small accounts are unglamorous — replying to every comment in the first hour, spending fifteen minutes engaging inside your niche tags before you post (which also teaches you what those communities actually reward), collaborating with accounts your size, and posting on a schedule your audience can learn. Hashtags make each of those efforts land in the right place. A post with warm early engagement from your community, filed under tags where that engagement level ranks, is the entire engine.

Mistakes small accounts make most

Copying the sets of the biggest account in the niche — their tags work at their scale and bury you at yours. Using all thirty slots because they exist; as the per-platform numbers show, a tight set of 5–10 true tags beats a stuffed one. Chasing trending tags unrelated to the post, which earns a burst of indifferent viewers and teaches the platform your content disappoints people. And buying followers or joining engagement pods, which poisons the very engagement-rate signals your hashtag ranking depends on — platforms detect both, and recovery is slow.

How your sets should grow with you

Think of your tag library as having weight classes. At under 1,000 followers, live almost entirely under 50,000-post tags. Crossing a few thousand followers with steady engagement, mid-sized tags (up to a few hundred thousand posts) become realistic ranking targets. Past 10,000, you can hold a genuinely mixed set and let one or two big tags ride along for classification. The signal to move up a class is never the follower count itself — it is your typical first-day engagement. When your average post would have ranked under the bigger tag last month, move. Growing accounts that re-test their ceiling every month or two consistently out-grow accounts that set a strategy once and freeze it.

TikTok vs Instagram Hashtags: What Is Different?

Creators who post the same video to TikTok and Instagram often paste the same hashtag block into both — and quietly underperform on both. The two platforms may look similar on the surface (vertical video, captions, tags underneath), but their discovery engines reward different things, and hashtags plug into those engines in different places. This guide breaks down the real differences and shows how to adapt one topic into two effective sets.

Two different discovery engines

Instagram grew up as a social graph: you followed accounts and hashtags, and discovery meant being filed where followers of a topic would look. Even now, with recommendations dominant, hashtags on Instagram still function as a genuine filing and search system — people follow tags, browse tag pages, and search them when planning, shopping and researching. A well-chosen Instagram tag puts you on a shelf that humans actively walk past.

TikTok's For You feed was never built on following. It assembles an audience for each video from behavioural signals — watch time, rewatches, shares, the sounds and topics a viewer lingers on. Hashtags there are a weaker categorisation hint for the algorithm and, increasingly, an entry in TikTok's growing search index. People do search TikTok heavily now, especially younger users researching products, places and how-tos — but they search in natural phrases, and that changes how a useful TikTok tag reads.

How the tags themselves should differ

On Instagram, tags can be community language: the compound, insider tags a niche uses for itself (#slowfashionmovement, #bookstagramuk) work because community members browse and follow them. Descriptive precision wins; a set of 5–10 mixed-size tags tailored to the post is the current sweet spot, as detailed in our per-platform guide.

On TikTok, write tags the way a stranger would type them into a search bar: plain topic phrases, the problem the video solves, the thing shown. Insider compounds still help categorise, but the traffic-bearing tags are the searchable ones. Three to six is plenty — caption space on TikTok does double duty, because the caption text itself is indexed for search, so a keyword-rich sentence beats three extra tags.

One more difference: trend mechanics. On TikTok, a tag attached to a rising sound or format can carry a video far if the video genuinely belongs to that trend — and does nothing, or slightly harms trust, when it doesn't. Instagram's trending tags are slower-moving and more topical. Chasing trends is a TikTok-native tactic; on Instagram, evergreen accuracy compounds better.

Adapting one topic into two sets

Take a single post — say, a 30-second video of a small apartment balcony garden. The Instagram set leans community and compound: the broad topic tag, the small-space gardening community tags, the city or climate tag, the specific plants shown, the aesthetic tag the community uses — eight or so tags, mixed sizes, exactly as the generator produces with Instagram selected. The TikTok version of the same video keeps maybe four: the searchable phrases someone would type ("small balcony garden", "grow vegetables in pots" rendered as tags), one broad tag, and the format tag only if it fits. Same video, two labelling philosophies: one for a shelf browsers walk past, one for a search box and a behavioural engine.

What stays the same

For all the differences, the foundations are shared. Relevance beats volume on both platforms — a tag that lies about the content earns indifferent viewers and throttles distribution on either. Restricted and spam-associated tags are a hazard on both, and worth the same one-minute check described in Banned Hashtags: How to Check and Avoid Them. Rotation matters on both: identical blocks under every post decay. And on both platforms, hashtags remain the garnish rather than the meal — the first two seconds of the video decide more than any tag ever will.

The practical workflow is simple: generate once with your topic, then use the platform selector to rebuild the set for each destination rather than copying one block everywhere. It takes twenty extra seconds per platform, and it is the difference between tags that were written for the room you are actually standing in and tags written for a room you left an hour ago.

Is the hashtag generator really free?

Yes. The generator is free to use as often as you like, with no account, no sign-up and no limits. It runs entirely in your browser — the hashtag lists are built into the page — so there is nothing to install and nothing to pay for.

How does the generator choose my hashtags?

It combines two sources. For well-known topics it draws on curated lists we maintain by hand, organised into broad, mid-sized and niche tags. For any keyword it also builds smart variations — compounds and community-style forms of your exact words. The platform you pick then shapes the mix: TikTok sets come out shorter and more search-like, X sets use CamelCase, and YouTube sets lead with your three strongest topic tags.

How many hashtags should I actually post?

It depends on the platform. As a working rule: 3–10 on Instagram feed posts, 3–5 on Reels, 3–6 on TikTok, 3–5 on YouTube and 1–2 on X. Generating a larger set and trimming it to the tags that truly fit your post is a good workflow. The full reasoning is in How Many Hashtags Should You Use in 2026?

Do hashtags still work in 2026?

Yes — as a labelling and discovery tool, not as a magic growth switch. Platforms now distribute posts mainly by predicted interest, but hashtags still file your post into topic shelves and search results where interested people find it. A relevant post with accurate tags will consistently out-discover the same post with sloppy ones.

Can a hashtag get my account shadowbanned?

A single restricted hashtag can suppress that post's distribution without any warning, and repeatedly using flagged tags alongside spam-like behaviour can reduce an account's reach more broadly. Our generator's curated lists avoid known restricted tags, but lists age — learn to check a tag yourself; it takes under a minute.

Should I put hashtags in the caption or the first comment?

On Instagram both placements perform equivalently for most accounts today, so choose whichever keeps your caption cleaner to read. On other platforms, hashtags belong in the caption or description itself.

Why do I get different results when I shuffle?

Because for most topics the pool of good tags is larger than the set you asked for. Shuffling draws a different mix from the same layered pool, which is handy for building the two or three rotating sets we recommend instead of repeating one identical block under every post.

What if my topic is not one of the popular ones?

Type it anyway. The variation engine works for any keyword — the more specific your keyword ("trail running", "sourdough baking", "street photography"), the more rankable niche tags it can build. Try pairing your topic with a place, a style or an audience for even better compounds.

Do you store the keywords I type?

No. The tool runs entirely on your device; nothing you type is sent to or stored on a server. See our Privacy Policy for the full picture.
